\abstract{When the density of noise hits in a tracking detector becomes large compared with
its resolution, some random combinations of hits are indistinguishable from real tracks:
they pass the same goodness-of-fit cut that real tracks pass, and no reconstruction
algorithm that keeps the efficiency for real tracks can reject them. We give their expected
number, which is therefore a lower limit on the fake-track rate of any algorithm. We first
treat a few parallel planes with uniform noise and straight tracks. The expected number is
the product of the numbers of noise hits and the probability that one random combination
passes the cut, and that probability, which can be as small as $10^{-23}$, follows from the
geometry: it is the number of distinguishable tracks in the acceptance, times the volume of a
$\chi^2$ sphere, divided by the volume of the space of hit positions, all in units of the
resolution. We then give the general solution, for layers of any shape and orientation
(cylinders, tilted planes, disks), helical tracks in a solenoidal field, noise densities that
vary across the detector, and tracks required to have hits on at least $k$ of $N$ layers. The
results are checked against direct Monte Carlo and brute-force counting in small detectors.}

\keywords{Pattern recognition, cluster finding, calibration and fitting methods; Performance of High Energy Physics Detectors; Analysis and statistical methods}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Track reconstruction in a high-occupancy environment must find the few hits left by charged
particles among many unrelated ones. The problem is familiar at hadron colliders, where
pile-up fills the inner layers~\cite{CMS:2014tracking,ATLAS:2017dense}, and it is extreme at
a muon collider, where the decay products of the circulating muons produce a beam-induced
background of millions of hits per bunch crossing in the
tracker~\cite{Accettura:2023,IMCC:2024interim,Casarsa:2024review,Bartosik:2020,Collamati:2021}.
Some combinations of background hits happen to line up well enough to be accepted as tracks.
The resulting fake-track rate is normally measured by running a specific reconstruction
algorithm on fully simulated events~\cite{Mankel:2004,Strandlie:2010,Fruhwirth:2021book}, so
the number obtained belongs to that algorithm.

This paper asks a different question: at what hit density does track reconstruction become
impossible for \emph{any} algorithm? The answer exists because a combination of noise hits
that fits a track hypothesis within the resolution carries no information distinguishing it
from a real track. An algorithm that keeps the real tracks that pass a given goodness-of-fit
requirement must also keep these combinations. Their expected number is therefore a lower
limit on the fake rate of every algorithm with that efficiency, and it can be computed from
the detector geometry, the resolution and the hit densities alone.

The expected number is the product of the numbers of noise hits and the probability $P$
that one random combination passes the cut. The difficulty is that $P$ is tiny, of order
$10^{-23}$ for six layers of realistic size, far beyond direct simulation. Earlier
work~\cite{Casarsa:2025jinst} (section~8.5) estimated such probabilities with a Monte Carlo at
inflated resolution, scaled back by the product of the resolution ratios. Here we make that
argument exact: $P$ follows a power law in the cut, $P\simeq Kc^{\ndof/2}$, whose exponent is
fixed by the number of degrees of freedom and whose constant $K$ is a geometric quantity, the
volume of the set of accepted tracks in the space of hit positions.

Related ideas appear in several fields. The probability that $k$ random points are aligned
within a strip of width $w$ scales as $(w/L)^{k-2}$~\cite{Kendall:1980,Broadbent:1980}; our
result contains this law as a special case. The ``number of false alarms'' of a~contrario
detection in image analysis~\cite{Desolneux:2000} is, like our estimate, an expected number of
chance events that needs no independence assumption. The volume of a tube around a manifold,
which gives our constant, was computed by Hotelling and Weyl~\cite{Hotelling:1939,Weyl:1939}
and is used in statistics for significance tests in nonlinear
models~\cite{Johansen:1990,Kuriki:2005}. Methods that make a rare event common by changing a
scale and then extrapolate back are used in structural
reliability~\cite{Bucher:2009,Naess:2009}; here the rescaling is an exact symmetry and nothing
is extrapolated by a fit. In particle physics, accidental coincidences between
counters~\cite{Leo:1994} and fake roads in track
triggers~\cite{Ashmanskas:2004svt,Annovi:2013rd13,Ryd:2020trig} are estimated with the same
product-of-occupancies reasoning, but to our knowledge no exact general expression has been
given for combinations accepted by a $\chi^2$ cut.

Section~\ref{sec:simple} introduces the problem and the method on the simplest case: a few
parallel planes, uniform noise and straight tracks, with numerical examples.
Section~\ref{sec:general} gives the general solution, for any detector surfaces, helical
tracks in a solenoidal field and noise densities that vary across the detector.
Section~\ref{sec:validation} checks the results against direct Monte Carlo and
brute-force counting, and section~\ref{sec:limits} discusses the limitations. An application
to the tracker of a muon collider is the subject of a companion paper~\todo{ref}.

\section{The simple case: parallel planes, uniform noise, straight tracks}\label{sec:simple}

\subsection{Detector, noise and acceptance}\label{sec:defs}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.72\textwidth]{planes_sketch}
  \caption{The simple case: six parallel square planes at depths $Y_i$, of side $W_i$ (the
  projective tower of section~\ref{sec:example}), each with a few uniformly distributed noise
  hits (grey). A straight track (red) crosses every plane; each plane measures the two
  coordinates $u$ and $v$ of its hit.}
  \label{fig:planes}
\end{figure}

The detector consists of $N$ parallel planes at depths $Y_i$ (figure~\ref{fig:planes}). Plane $i$ measures two
coordinates $u$ and $v$ of each hit, with Gaussian resolutions $\sigma_{u,i}$ and
$\sigma_{v,i}$, over a region of area $A_i$ (in the examples a square of side $W_i$). Noise
hits are distributed uniformly over each plane, independently of each other, with $n_i$ hits
on plane $i$ (a fixed number, or a Poisson variable of mean $n_i$; the results are the same).
Tracks are straight lines, $u=a_u+b_uY$ and $v=a_v+b_vY$.

A combination of one hit per plane is fitted by least squares to the track model, with $p=4$
parameters; with $m=2N$ measured coordinates the fit has $\ndof=m-p$ degrees of freedom. The
combination is accepted as a track if $\chi^2<c$ and the fitted parameters lie in an
acceptance region $\Theta$. The cut $c$ is set by the efficiency required for real tracks; we
use a one-sided $3\sigma$ cut, $\Pr(\chi^2_{\ndof}>c)=1.35\times10^{-3}$, which gives
$c=17.80$, $21.74$ and $25.36$ for $\ndof=4$, $6$ and $8$.

\subsection{The counting identity and the lower limit}\label{sec:limit}

Let $P$ be the probability that one combination of independent, uniformly distributed hits
is accepted. The expected number of accepted noise combinations is
\begin{equation}
  E = P\prod_{i=1}^{N} n_i ,
  \label{eq:identity}
\end{equation}
by linearity of expectation: $E$ is the sum, over the $\prod_i n_i$ combinations, of the
probability that each is accepted. The identity is exact for the mean even though
combinations that share hits are correlated; correlations affect the variance of the number
of fakes, not its mean.

A combination of noise hits that passes the cut is, as data, a valid track: some particle
with parameters in $\Theta$ could have produced those hits with the observed residuals.
Consider any algorithm that accepts every combination of hits compatible with a track in the
acceptance, $\chi^2<c$ and $\theta\in\Theta$, so that its efficiency for real tracks is at
least that of the cut. Such an algorithm accepts all the noise combinations counted by
eq.~\eqref{eq:identity}, and $E$ is a lower limit on its number of fake tracks. An algorithm
can produce fewer fakes, but only by also losing real tracks: by tightening $c$ or $\Theta$,
which the same formula quantifies, or by rejecting candidates according to their
surroundings, which removes real tracks in the same surroundings. When $E$ approaches one per
event, the information needed to reconstruct tracks with the stated efficiency is no longer in
the data.

The limit counts only combinations made entirely of noise hits. Combinations in which some
hits belong to a real track and others are noise are also fakes in the usual sense; they only
add to the number given here.

\subsection{The probability that one combination passes}\label{sec:method}

\paragraph{Whitened coordinates.} Dividing every measured coordinate by its resolution turns
the weighted fit into an unweighted one. A combination of hits becomes a point $x$ in an
$m$-dimensional space, uniformly distributed in a box $B$ whose sides are $W_i/\sigma_{u,i}$
and $W_i/\sigma_{v,i}$, of volume $|B|=\prod_i A_i/(\sigma_{u,i}\sigma_{v,i})$. Each track
$\theta$ produces an ideal point $f(\theta)$, and as $\theta$ runs over the accepted tracks
whose hits lie inside every plane, these points form a $p$-dimensional surface $M$, the
\emph{track manifold}. The fit finds the point of $M$ nearest to $x$, and
\begin{equation}
  \chi^2(x) = \min_{\theta}\,\lvert x-f(\theta)\rvert^2
\end{equation}
is the squared distance from $x$ to $M$. In these coordinates every plane is a box and every
acceptance region a sphere, whatever the sizes and resolutions.

\paragraph{The small-$\chi^2$ law.} The accepted combinations are the points within distance
$\sqrt c$ of $M$: a thin tube around the track manifold. In each of the $\ndof$ directions
perpendicular to $M$ the tube is a sphere of radius $\sqrt c$, of volume $V_{\ndof}c^{\ndof/2}$
with $V_d=\pi^{d/2}/\Gamma(d/2+1)$. To leading order its volume is the area of $M$ times this
sphere volume, the result of Hotelling and Weyl~\cite{Hotelling:1939,Weyl:1939}, and since $x$
is uniform in $B$,
\begin{equation}
  P \simeq K\,c^{\ndof/2},\qquad K = \frac{V_{\ndof}\,\Vol(M)}{|B|},\qquad
  \Vol(M)=\int_{\Theta_B}\sqrt{\det\!\left(J^\mathsf{T}J\right)}\;\dd^p\theta ,
  \label{eq:K}
\end{equation}
where $J=\partial f/\partial\theta$ is the $m\times p$ matrix of derivatives of the whitened
ideal hits with respect to the track parameters, and $\Theta_B$ is the set of parameters in
$\Theta$ whose ideal hits all lie inside the planes (appendix~\ref{app:tube}).

The exponent depends only on the number of degrees of freedom. The constant has a simple
reading: $J^\mathsf{T}J$ is the inverse covariance matrix $C^{-1}$ of the fitted parameters, so
$\Vol(M)=\int\dd^p\theta/\sqrt{\det C}$ is the number of \emph{distinguishable tracks} in the
acceptance, the volume of parameter space counted in units of the parameter errors. The
expected number of fakes is this number of tracks times the probability that, for one of
them, random hits fall inside its $\chi^2$ sphere on every plane.

For straight lines the Jacobian is constant: $\det(J^\mathsf{T}J)$ is the product over the two
views of $(N\sum_iY_i^2-(\sum_iY_i)^2)/\sigma^4$, and $\Theta_B$ is, in each view, the polygon of
$(a,b)$ for which $|a+bY_i|\le W_i/2$ on every plane. The integral reduces to the area of that
polygon, a one-dimensional integral. The familiar small-argument form of the $\chi^2$
distribution, $\Pr(\chi^2_{\ndof}<c)\simeq(c/2)^{\ndof/2}/\Gamma(\ndof/2+1)$ for Gaussian
residuals~\cite{DLMF}, has the same power for the same geometric reason, with a different
constant because noise hits are uniform rather than Gaussian around a track. For $k$ points in
one view, eq.~\eqref{eq:K} reproduces the $(w/L)^{k-2}$ law of chance
alignments~\cite{Kendall:1980,Broadbent:1980}.

\paragraph{Corrections.} Equation~\eqref{eq:K} holds when the sphere is small compared with the
planes in whitened units. The leading corrections, from the corners of the acceptance region
and from the curvature of $M$, are of relative order $c$ in units of the whitened plane size;
for a linear track model and a flat boundary the first-order edge effects cancel
(appendix~\ref{app:tube}). In any realistic detector the planes are hundreds to thousands of
resolutions wide and the corrections are negligible.

\subsection{Resolution scaling and exact rescaling}\label{sec:scaling}

At fixed geometry, $\Vol(M)\propto\sigma^{-p}$ and $|B|\propto\sigma^{-m}$, so at fixed cut
\begin{equation}
  P\propto\sigma^{\ndof},
  \label{eq:sigma}
\end{equation}
and $P\propto\sigma_u^{n_u}\sigma_v^{n_v}$ when the two views have different resolutions and
$n_u+n_v=\ndof$ degrees of freedom. Equivalently, shrinking every length of the detector by a
factor $\lambda$ at fixed resolution multiplies every residual by $1/\lambda$ and $\chi^2$ by
$1/\lambda^2$, so that the probability in the real detector is exactly that in the shrunken one
at a rescaled cut,
\begin{equation}
  P_\mathrm{true}(c)=P_\mathrm{shrunk}(c/\lambda^2).
  \label{eq:rescale}
\end{equation}
We use this identity to test eq.~\eqref{eq:K} by direct Monte Carlo: a detector shrunk until
its smallest plane is two resolutions wide makes accepted combinations common. We call it the
reference detector and $c'=c/\lambda^2$ the cut mapped onto it.

\subsection{Requiring at least $k$ of $N$ planes}\label{sec:kofn}

Real detectors are inefficient, and track finders usually accept candidates with hits on at
least $k$ of the $N$ planes. Each set $S$ of planes with $|S|\ge k$ then defines its own fit,
with its own number of degrees of freedom and its own cut at the same efficiency for real
tracks: for five of six planes, $\ndof=8$ for the full set and $6$ for each of the six subsets
of five planes. By the same linearity argument,
\begin{equation}
  E(\ge k) = \sum_{|S|\ge k} P_S\prod_{i\in S} n_i ,
  \label{eq:kofn}
\end{equation}
where $P_S$ is the probability for the planes of $S$ alone, computed for their own geometry.
Each term with fewer planes has a larger probability and one factor $n_i$ less; when the $n_i$
are large but the resolution is good, the terms with the fewest planes dominate, by orders of
magnitude (section~\ref{sec:example}).

The sum counts combinations, not distinct fake tracks. A six-hit fake usually contains five-hit
subsets that also pass, and two five-hit fakes can share four hits; any algorithm would merge
such candidates. The size of the double counting is set by the \emph{window occupancy} $\eta$:
for an accepted candidate, the expected number of other hits that could replace one of its hits
and still pass, summed over its planes. For plane $i$ of the set,
\begin{equation}
  \eta_i \simeq (n_i-1)\,\frac{\pi\,(c/2)\,\sqrt{(\sigma_{u,i}^2+V_{u,i})(\sigma_{v,i}^2+V_{v,i})}}{A_i},
  \label{eq:eta}
\end{equation}
where $V_{u,i}$, $V_{v,i}$ are the variances of the track position predicted on plane $i$ by
the fit to the other planes, and $c/2$ is the mean $\chi^2$ budget left to the extra hit. If the
number of such neighbours is Poisson with mean $\eta$, the number of distinct fakes is the number
of combinations times $1-\eta/2+O(\eta^2)$, and we find that
\begin{equation}
  E_\mathrm{distinct}\simeq e^{-\eta/2}\,E(\ge k)
  \label{eq:distinct}
\end{equation}
describes the straight-line case within a few per cent up to $\eta\approx2$
(section~\ref{sec:validation}). It is exact as $\eta\to0$, which is the regime of interest.

\subsection{A numerical example}\label{sec:example}

Consider six square planes at $Y=250$, $500$, \dots, $1500$~mm, forming a projective tower with
an outermost plane of $1~\mathrm{m}^2$ ($W_i=Y_i/1.5$), and $\sigma=100~\mu$m in both
coordinates. Equation~\eqref{eq:K} gives, for six of six planes, $K=2.0\times10^{-29}$ and
$P=Kc^4=8.2\times10^{-24}$ at $c=25.36$; the tower contains about
$1.2\times10^{15}$ distinguishable straight tracks. For each of the six five-plane subsets, $P$
lies between $2.0\times10^{-18}$ and $1.3\times10^{-17}$.

With a uniform noise density of $10^{-3}$ hits/mm$^2$ on every plane (from 28 hits on the
smallest plane to 1000 on the largest), the expected number of fakes is $2.0\times10^{-9}$ with
six of six planes and $4.1\times10^{-5}$ with at least five of six: the five-plane terms
dominate by four orders of magnitude. Figure~\ref{fig:simple} shows how $E$ grows with the
density and with the resolution. Since the dominant terms scale as $\rho^5$ and $\sigma^6$, one
expected fake is reached at a density that falls roughly as $\sigma^{-6/5}$: $4.0\times10^{-2}$,
$1.7\times10^{-2}$, $7.6\times10^{-3}$ and $3.3\times10^{-3}$ hits/mm$^2$ for $\sigma=25$, $50$,
$100$ and $200~\mu$m.

\section{The general solution}\label{sec:general}

\subsection{Arbitrary surfaces and local densities}\label{sec:surfaces}

Nothing in the argument of section~\ref{sec:method} requires the layers to be flat or parallel,
or the noise to be uniform. Let each layer be a surface on which a hit measures two local
coordinates, with resolutions $\sigma_{u,i}$ and $\sigma_{v,i}$, and let the noise density per
unit area on layer $i$ be $\rho_i(x)$, which may vary across the layer. A track $\theta$ crosses
layer $i$ at a point $x_i(\theta)$, with local coordinates that, divided by the resolutions,
form the whitened ideal point $f(\theta)$, as before. The expected number of noise combinations
on a set $S$ of layers that pass the cut is the integral, over the positions of the hits, of
the product of the densities times the indicator that the combination passes. To leading order
the passing combinations again fill a tube of radius $\sqrt c$ around the track manifold, over
which the densities are constant, and
\begin{equation}
  E_S = V_{\ndof}\,c^{\ndof/2}\int_{\Theta_S}\dd^p\theta\;\sqrt{\det\!\left(J_S^\mathsf{T}J_S\right)}
        \;\prod_{i\in S}\rho_i\bigl(x_i(\theta)\bigr)\,\sigma_{u,i}\sigma_{v,i} ,
  \label{eq:general}
\end{equation}
where $J_S$ is the matrix of derivatives of the whitened local crossing coordinates on the
layers of $S$, and $\Theta_S$ the set of accepted tracks that cross the active area of every
layer of $S$. The volume of the box no longer appears: each density enters where the track
crosses its layer. For uniform densities, $\rho_i=n_i/A_i$, eq.~\eqref{eq:general} reduces to
$P_S\prod_{i\in S}n_i$ with $P_S$ from eq.~\eqref{eq:K}.

The shape and orientation of each layer enter only through $J_S$. A layer crossed at a grazing
angle spreads the crossing points of nearby tracks over a larger area, increasing
$\sqrt{\det(J_S^\mathsf{T}J_S)}$: there are more distinguishable tracks per unit of hit density.
Cylinders, planes at any tilt, disks and their combinations are treated alike.

\subsection{Helical tracks in a solenoidal field}\label{sec:field}

In a uniform axial field we take tracks from the beam line, with parameters
$\theta=(\phi_0,\kappa,z_0,t)$: initial azimuth, signed curvature, longitudinal origin and
$t=\cot\vartheta$. At transverse arc length $s$ the track is at
\begin{equation}
  x+iy = s\,\mathrm{sinc}(\kappa s/2)\,e^{i(\phi_0+\kappa s/2)},\qquad z=z_0+ts ,
\end{equation}
with $\mathrm{sinc}(h)=\sin h/h$. The crossing with a coaxial cylinder of radius $R$ has the
closed form $s=(2/\kappa)\arcsin(\kappa R/2)$, at azimuth $\phi_0+\arcsin(\kappa R/2)$; the
crossing with a plane of any orientation is found by a few Newton iterations in $s$. The
derivatives of the crossing coordinates follow analytically by implicit differentiation, so
$J_S$ costs no more than the crossing itself. The fit has $p=4$ parameters and
$\ndof=2|S|-4$. The acceptance requires $|\kappa|\le\kappa_\mathrm{max}$, a minimum transverse
momentum $p_T>0.3\,B/\kappa_\mathrm{max}$ (GeV/$c$, tesla, metres), and an origin within the
luminous region: we assume a longitudinal beam spot of $\sigma_z=1.5$~mm, the bunch length foreseen
at the interaction point of a 10~TeV muon collider~\cite{IMCC:2024interim}, and require
$|z_0|<3\sigma_z=4.5$~mm.

The track manifold is now curved, but eq.~\eqref{eq:general} still holds to leading order; the
curvature adds corrections of relative order $c$, as the edges do. For the fits needed in the
validation we minimize $\chi^2$ with the Levenberg--Marquardt
method~\cite{Levenberg:1944,Marquardt:1963}, started from several values of the curvature; a
single starting point would occasionally stop at a local minimum, overestimate $\chi^2$ and
underestimate the number of fakes.

Shrinking every length by $\lambda$ maps a helix of curvature $\kappa$ onto one of curvature
$\lambda\kappa$; with $\kappa_\mathrm{max}\to\lambda\kappa_\mathrm{max}$ the family of accepted
tracks is mapped onto itself and eq.~\eqref{eq:rescale} remains exact.

\subsection{At least $k$ of $N$ layers in a general detector}\label{sec:kofn-general}

In a real detector different tracks cross different layers: a track may cross some
cylinders coaxial with the beam and some disks perpendicular to it. Equation~\eqref{eq:kofn}
generalizes to a single integral over the track parameters, in which each track contributes the
sum over the subsets of the layers it actually crosses,
\begin{equation}
  E(\ge k) = \int\dd^p\theta\;w(\theta),\qquad
  w(\theta)=\sum_{\substack{S\subseteq C(\theta)\\|S|\ge k}} V_{\ndof,S}\,c_S^{\ndof,S/2}
  \sqrt{\det\!\left(J_S^\mathsf{T}J_S\right)}\prod_{i\in S}\rho_i\,\sigma_{u,i}\sigma_{v,i},
  \label{eq:w}
\end{equation}
where $C(\theta)$ is the set of layers crossed by track $\theta$ and each subset has its own
cut. Overlapping modules within a layer, gaps and mixed combinations of cylinders and disks need no
special treatment: they are all accounted for by $C(\theta)$.

\subsection{Evaluating the integral}\label{sec:sampling}

The integral of eq.~\eqref{eq:w} is evaluated by sampling track parameters uniformly over a
region containing the acceptance, computing for each sampled track the layers it crosses, the
derivatives $J$ and the densities at the crossings, and averaging $w(\theta)$; the volume of the
sampled region times the mean of $w$ is $E$. Each sampled track carries its own contribution,
$w(\theta)$, the density of fakes at that point of track-parameter space, so the same sample
gives the distribution of the expected fakes over the detector: in polar angle, along the beam,
by layer combination or by region. Symmetries reduce the work: with densities independent of
azimuth, $\phi_0$ needs to be sampled only over a small range. The densities $\rho_i(x)$ can be
taken directly from a simulation of the background, layer by layer and, where needed, as
functions of position on the layer.

\section{Validation}\label{sec:validation}

We test the results in small detectors where everything can be counted, with $\sigma=0.1$~mm in
both coordinates.

\paragraph{Parallel planes.} Projective towers of four and six planes at $Y_i\propto i$, with
sides $W_i\propto Y_i$, for straight lines (figure~\ref{fig:val-line}) and for helices
(figure~\ref{fig:val-helix}; in this test the depth view is written as $z=p+qr$, with $r$ the
transverse distance, which differs from the path length by less than $0.2\%$). For towers of
growing size (smaller $c'$) we measure $P$ directly, by fitting random combinations until
several hundred pass, and divide it by $Kc'^{\,\ndof/2}$ with $K$ from eq.~\eqref{eq:K}. The
ratio tends to one as $c'\to0$. For straight lines with four planes it is consistent with one
within the statistical errors of $\pm5\%$ for $c'$ from $0.12$ down to $0.004$, where
$P=3.7\times10^{-7}$. For the helix with four planes it is $0.973$, $0.986$, $1.020$ and $1.013$
($\pm0.02$--$0.03$) at $c'=0.070$, $0.031$, $0.017$ and $0.0077$, down to $P=3.5\times10^{-7}$; the
six-plane points, down to $P=4\times10^{-8}$, follow the same trend. At larger $c'$ the ratio
falls below one, as expected from the finite size of the sphere.

\paragraph{General surfaces.} Four detectors of four layers each, of growing size $L$
(figures~\ref{fig:gsurf-sketch} and~\ref{fig:val-general}): coaxial cylinders of equal length; planes tilted
by $45^\circ$ in the transverse plane; planes tilted by $60^\circ$ towards the beam axis; and two
cylinders followed by two disks perpendicular to the beam. Exact helices are fitted to random combinations of hits on the four surfaces and the
passing fraction is compared with eq.~\eqref{eq:general}, computed by sampling. The ratio tends to
one as the detectors grow: $0.95\pm0.05$ and $1.17\pm0.10$ for the two largest sets of cylinders,
$1.04\pm0.05$ and $0.99\pm0.08$ for the $45^\circ$ planes, $1.09\pm0.05$ and $1.06\pm0.09$ for the
$60^\circ$ planes, and $0.87\pm0.04$ and $0.91\pm0.08$ for the mixed detector, where the
non-projective geometry makes the finite-size corrections larger. Applied to the parallel-plane
helix tower above, the same code reproduces $K$ to $0.998\pm0.007$.

\paragraph{The counting identity.} We generate noise-only events and fit \emph{every}
combination: one hit on each of six planes, and one hit on each plane of every five-plane subset,
each with its own cut, and compare the numbers that pass with $P_S\prod_{i\in S}n_i$. For
straight lines an exact pruning of the combinatorics (a partial combination already above the
cut cannot pass) allows up to 1000 hits per plane and probabilities $P_S$ down to $10^{-15}$; in
six configurations, with 8 to 1000 hits per plane, the ratio of counted to predicted combinations
is between $0.975$ and $1.037$, and $0.990\pm0.008$ combined. For helices every combination is
fitted by brute force, with 5 to 10 hits per plane; the ratio is $0.97\pm0.03$ over three
configurations.

\paragraph{Distinct fakes.} In the same events, candidates that share four or more hits are
merged and the distinct candidates counted. For straight lines the ratio of distinct fakes to
combinations follows $e^{-\eta/2}$, eq.~\eqref{eq:distinct}, within $1$--$5\%$ for
$0.17\le\eta\le2.2$. For helices it falls somewhat lower at large occupancy, by a fraction of
about $0.1\,\eta$, and approaches the same relation as $\eta$ decreases.

\paragraph{Why the constant is computed, not fitted.} One could instead fit $K$ to a Monte Carlo
on the reference detector, with a low-order polynomial for the finite-$c'$ corrections. We find
that such fits are biased by a few per cent for straight lines and by up to $10\%$ for helices,
and that the result depends on the fitted range and on the order of the polynomial.
Equations~\eqref{eq:K} and~\eqref{eq:general} have no such freedom; their numerical integration
is precise to $10^{-4}$ for the towers and to better than $1\%$ by sampling.

\section{Uncertainties and limitations}\label{sec:limits}

\paragraph{Numerical.} The integrals are computed to $10^{-4}$ for the parallel-plane towers and
to better than $1\%$ by sampling; the finite-$c$ corrections are negligible for detectors of
realistic size. The validation tests the complete chain at the level of a few per cent, limited
by Monte Carlo statistics.

\paragraph{Assumptions.} The resolution is taken as Gaussian without tails, and multiple
scattering is neglected, which is adequate for high-momentum tracks. The noise density must vary
slowly on the scale of the fit windows, a few resolutions. The tracks come from the beam line;
other track families (displaced tracks, cosmic rays) need their own parameters but no new
formula.

\paragraph{What the limit does not include.} Combinations mixing hits of real tracks with noise,
correlated noise (clusters of hits from one particle, low-momentum particles curling in the field)
and inefficiency of real tracks all add fakes or remove real tracks; none can lower the limit.

\section{Conclusions}\label{sec:conclusions}

The expected number of combinations of noise hits that pass the goodness-of-fit cut of a track fit
is a lower limit on the fake-track rate of any reconstruction algorithm that keeps the efficiency
for real tracks given by that cut and acceptance. It is the product of the noise densities and a
geometric factor: the number of distinguishable tracks in the acceptance times the volume of the
$\chi^2$ sphere, everything in units of the resolution. The result holds for layers of any shape and
orientation, for helical tracks in a solenoidal field and for noise densities that vary across the
detector, and is evaluated by a low-dimensional integral over the track parameters, with no Monte
Carlo of the rare event itself.

The limit grows as the $k$-th power of a common hit density for tracks with at least $k$ of $N$
layers, and as the $\ndof$-th power of the resolution. When hits may be missing, the sets with the
fewest layers dominate, by orders of magnitude. For six planes of $1~\mathrm{m}^2$ scale and
$100~\mu$m resolution, one fake per event is reached at a noise density of about
$8\times10^{-3}$ hits/mm$^2$. The method gives this kind of statement for any detector from its
geometry, resolution and expected occupancy alone, and can be used early in the design of a
tracker to set requirements on resolution and background rejection.

\section{The volume of the tube}\label{app:tube}

\paragraph{The tube.} Near the track manifold, write each point of the hit space as
$x=f(\theta)+\sum_{j=1}^{\ndof}t_j\,n_j(\theta)$, with $n_j(\theta)$ an orthonormal basis of the
directions perpendicular to $M$ at $f(\theta)$. For a point close to $M$, $\chi^2(x)=|t|^2$, and
the fitted parameters are $\theta$. The volume element is
$\dd^mx=\sqrt{\det(J^\mathsf{T}J)}\,(1+O(|t|/R))\,\dd^p\theta\,\dd^{\ndof}t$, with $R$ the radius of
curvature of $M$. Integrating $t$ over the sphere $|t|<\sqrt c$ and $\theta$ over the acceptance,
with the density of hit combinations evaluated at $f(\theta)$, gives eqs.~\eqref{eq:K}
and~\eqref{eq:general}. For a linear model $R=\infty$ and the expansion is exact away from the
boundary.

\paragraph{The boundary.} Near the edge of a layer, part of the sphere around a point of $M$ inside
the acceptance lies outside, and part of the sphere around a point just outside lies inside. For a
linear model and a flat edge, a displacement $t$ shifts the boundary of the accepted parameters
linearly in $t$, and the average over the sphere vanishes: the two effects cancel to first order.
The leading corrections come from the corners and from the curvature of $M$, and are of relative
order $c$ in units of the whitened layer size.

\paragraph{Curved layers.} On a curved layer the local coordinates are coordinates on the surface,
and the residuals are measured along it. When the radius of curvature of the layer is much larger
than the resolution, as for any real detector, the surface is flat on the scale of the $\chi^2$
sphere and the argument above applies unchanged.
